Core Principles

Discuss & Tailor: Senior clinician & radiologist discussion is vital. Tailor imaging to the individual child.

ALARP Principle: Increased radiation risk in children. Adhere strictly to 'As Low As Reasonably Practicable'.

CT is Not Mandatory: CT is helpful but should be used judiciously, not as a default for all trauma.

Avoid FAST/Unenhanced US: These can provide false reassurance in blunt abdominal trauma.

CT Exclusion Tool for Blunt Polytrauma (<16s)

Consider NOT scanning an anatomical region if it satisfies ALL criteria below. Senior clinical discretion is paramount.

1. Head

  • GCS not below 13, and not intubated
  • Does NOT trigger the NICE NG232 CT head criteria (under-16s algorithm)

2. Neck

  • GCS not below 13, and not intubated
  • Does NOT trigger the NICE NG232 cervical spine imaging criteria (under-16s algorithm)

3. Chest

  • Chest X-ray normal
  • Chest exam normal
  • NOT rapid deceleration (fall >6m or MVC >40mph)

4. Abdomen

  • No lap belt or handlebar injury
  • No abdominal wall ecchymosis
  • No abdominal tenderness (in conscious pt)
  • No abdominal distension
  • No persistent hypovolaemia (e.g. unexplained tachycardia)
  • No blood from the rectum or nasogastric tube (RCR section 4.2.20 also lists blood from the urethra)

5. Spine

  • No midline tenderness
  • Low concern on mechanism
  • No neurology
  • MRI should be first line where possible (and is the primary modality with definite neurological signs).

6. Limbs

  • No complex fractures (consider)
  • No concern of vascular injury (consider)

Plain radiographs of the injured region are first line, usually AP and lateral including the adjacent joints.

Wider concerns that could lower the threshold to CT:

  • Patient needs an emergency operation
  • Long secondary transfer required
  • Patient will be sedated in critical care for an extended period

Consider imaging to exclude injury when history or examination is compromised:

  • Decreased level of consciousness, including intubated patients
  • Unwitnessed injury in the non-verbal child
  • Limited clinical observation, e.g. awaiting transfer or needing immediate surgery
  • Suspected physical abuse: follow the RCR guidance The radiological investigation of suspected physical abuse in children

Key points by region (RCR section 4.2)

Head and cervical spine

  • Use the NICE NG232 under-16s algorithms for CT head and cervical spine imaging
  • 3D CT reconstruction of the skull vault is highly recommended (better detection of linear fractures)

Thoracic and lumbar spine

  • Plain radiographs of the injured region are generally the primary investigation
  • Targeted CT may be needed; the lumbar spine is included in CT abdomen and pelvis
  • Definite neurological signs: MRI where possible

Chest

  • Chest X-ray is the primary investigation
  • Consider contrast-enhanced CT with rapid deceleration (fall >6 m or MVC >40 mph), intoxication, reduced GCS or distracting painful injury
  • CT can be omitted if the chest film and examination are normal

Abdomen

  • Where indicated, contrast-enhanced CT abdomen and pelvis, single-volume dual-contrast
  • Hand injection of contrast is appropriate in very small children and babies
  • Isolated head injury: a reduced GCS alone should not justify abdominal CT

Pelvis

  • Pelvic fractures are rare; a screening pelvic X-ray is not indicated in all cases
  • A pelvic binder alone is not an indication for imaging without clinical assessment
  • If imaging after binder removal is needed, orthopaedics and the wider team should be present

Delayed presentation

  • Consider contrast-enhanced imaging, given the limits of non-enhanced imaging

† Bastion or modified Bastion protocol is the standard for contrast administration (choice per local practice).

‡ Consider CT angiogram in place of Bastion or modified Bastion if arterial injury is suspected.

§ Use wider coverage if the entry wound is at the junction of two body parts (e.g. base of neck and upper chest, or lower chest and upper abdomen), or the depth or track could cross between them.

Clinical assessment can underestimate the extent, depth and number of injuries; in hypovolaemic patients assume a greater level of trauma. Senior emergency physician and radiologist discussion should precede CT. Ask: "Is there any part of the body that we do not need to image?"

Dual-phase imaging may be needed for ongoing haemorrhage or intra-abdominal injury, but can be falsely reassuring because of vasoconstriction in children.

CT dose optimisation

  • Set mA, kVp and pitch by weight or age
  • Accept a level of noise appropriate for trauma scanning
  • Use iterative reconstruction, tube current or organ-based modulation where available
  • Use protocols that reduce dose to radiosensitive areas such as the lens
